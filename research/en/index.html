Research overview

What can be determined from limited information?

UCL uses mathematics to study how far limited information can determine an object’s state or properties, and the conclusions that follow. What information is sufficient when we do not know the object in full? Conversely, what loss of information makes determination impossible? Our research focuses on identifying these conditions and boundaries.

For example, can observations of only part of an object guarantee a property of the whole? When two different states produce the same observations, what else must be examined to distinguish them? Through such problems, we study how the amount and combination of available information relate to the conclusions that can be drawn.

We present our results as theorems, with explicit conditions and proofs. When a claim does not hold, we explain why through counterexamples or structural analysis. By working on individual problems, we deepen a mathematical understanding of information and of what can be determined from it, across problems of observation, identification, and verification.

Width-eleven strip

What sensor density is sufficient for a hexagonal strip eleven rows wide?

Overview

Sensors must distinguish every unselected vertex in an infinite strip eleven rows wide. This paper determines the exact minimum locating-dominating density: 48/143.

Details

The lower bound 48/143 holds for all locating-dominating sets, including nonperiodic ones. A 26-column periodic construction selects 96 of 286 vertices and attains equality. This settles width eleven in Gomes and Wakabayashi's Conjecture 9, not the conjecture at all widths.

002

Walks on the king grid

How many visits are needed to identify locations while moving, when revisits also count?

Overview

A walk on the king grid may move horizontally, vertically, or diagonally. Its visited vertices must form an identifying code. This paper bounds the number of visits, counting repeated visits to the same vertex.

Details

For an n×n board with n≥6, the minimum number of visits M_n satisfies 28n²/113 − 7n ≤ M_n ≤ n²/4 + 36n. The limit of M_n/n² exists and lies between 28/113 and 1/4. Whether the limit equals 1/4 remains open.

003

Error detection: king grid

How many detectors are needed when one detector's positive report may be lost?

Overview

Each detector senses the eight neighboring vertices of the king grid, excluding its own vertex. This study improves the density lower bound for DET:OLD sets under the specified model of a false-negative report at an unknown detector.

Details

Every DET:OLD set has lower limiting graph-ball density at least 79/192 at every center. Together with the known upper bound, the minimum lies between 79/192 and 13/30. No periodicity is assumed, and the exact minimum remains open.

004

Error detection: square grid

How many detectors are needed when reports distinguish a detector's own location from its neighbors?

Overview

On the square grid with four neighbors per vertex, a detector reports different values for its own location, an adjacent location, and all other locations. This paper improves the density lower bound when a nonzero report at one unknown detector may be lost.

Details

Every DET:LD set has lower limiting graph-ball density at least 168/295 at every center. With the known upper bound, the minimum lies between 168/295 and 7/12. The result includes nonperiodic sets; the exact minimum remains open.

005

Kagome grid

Can sensors distinguish every location in the kagome grid without sensing their own vertices?

Overview

In the kagome grid of triangles and hexagons, every vertex, including sensor locations, must have a distinct nonempty set of neighboring sensors. This paper establishes the exact minimum open-locating-dominating density of 2/5.

Details

The known universal lower bound 2/5 is explicitly transferred to the lower limiting graph-ball density of every OLD set at every center. A periodic construction selecting 12 of 30 vertices attains equality. The lower bound does not assume periodicity.
