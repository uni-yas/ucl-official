Which candidates are selected from a finite, discrete set, and under what conditions do they remain stable?

UCL is an interdisciplinary research program exploring this question through mathematics and computational experiments. It examines the structures that can be found, the criteria used to choose among competing candidates, and how those choices and structures hold up as conditions change.

Formal verification and reproducible procedures are central to this work. The aim is to make results verifiable by preserving the findings together with their assumptions, inputs, and methods, so that both the reasoning and the calculations can be traced and checked.

Potential applications of this research include materials design, software verification, production and logistics planning, equipment control, resource allocation, and the analysis of biological networks. Across these fields, some problems can be represented in terms of finite, discrete candidates or states, whose properties can be examined under specified constraints and update rules.

One cross-disciplinary research question is under what conditions fixed points—states that remain unchanged when a specified rule is applied—exist, and what procedures can reach them. By explicitly defining how candidate sets are narrowed or states are updated, and checking the results and the conditions under which they hold, we explore potential applications to search and decision-making.

Our aim is to use this understanding of structure to define the scope of a search and set priorities for verification, supporting decisions about where to allocate limited computing resources, research funding, and personnel.

  1. Applied and Discrete Mathematics

    Describing structure

    Candidate sets and states are represented mathematically to examine their relationships and properties. This provides a foundation for grouping equivalent structures and identifying forms that satisfy specified conditions.

  2. Optimization and Model Selection

    Defining how to choose

    Candidates are compared using criteria such as error, complexity, and the available computational budget. The aim is to make the basis for selection explicit and understand how different criteria affect the outcome.

  3. Formal Methods and Mathematical Logic

    Checking the reasoning

    Formal verification checks whether a conclusion follows from stated definitions and assumptions. The program has used Lean4 to verify steps in mathematical proofs by computer. The scope of these checks is the set of definitions and assumptions used in each proof.

  4. Signal Processing and Pattern Recognition

    Identifying patterns

    Periodic behavior, spectral components, and discrete patterns in data are analyzed to find regularities. This includes examining how reliably patterns can be identified or recovered when noise is introduced.

  5. Computational Science and Complex Systems

    Exploring changing conditions

    Computational experiments vary conditions to examine structural stability and the boundaries at which outcomes change. The focus is on identifying which conditions preserve a result and where a different outcome emerges.

  6. Reproducible Research and Scientific Software Engineering

    Making results checkable

    Inputs, computational procedures, execution environments, and results are recorded so that runs can be repeated and compared under the same conditions. Control experiments help distinguish what has been observed from the claims those observations can support.
